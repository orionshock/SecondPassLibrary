# The Narrative of Second Pass Library

Second Pass Library is a small, self-hosted library intended for a household or similarly close community. Its design begins not with software features, but with the story of entering a real library.

A new library begins with one room: the common room. It contains the shared collection and the people who use it. Readers come to browse, read, organize their experience, and leave notes in the margins.

At the librarian’s desk, the collection itself is cared for. Librarians import books, correct their metadata, and maintain the institution’s catalog. At the manager’s desk, the people of the library are cared for: managers govern users, participation, and access. Above them is the owner—not an entirely different kind of authority, but a manager with responsibility for the institution itself, including its identity and the appointment of other managers.

More advanced libraries may open additional rooms. These rooms are groups through which books, readers, and shared shelves are organized. The common room is not a separate implementation; it is simply the default room, operating under the same rules as every other group. Simple mode keeps the library to that one room, while advanced mode permits managers to create more rooms and arrange people and books among them.

A curator is a reader entrusted with the stewardship of a particular room. Curators may organize that group’s books and shelves, but their authority remains bounded by what they can already see. They do not receive the master keys to the institution merely because they have been asked to care for one room.

Authority therefore forms a capability ladder. A librarian satisfies the authority of a curator everywhere in the institution and adds the ability to import books and edit metadata. A manager satisfies librarian authority and may also manage people. The owner satisfies manager authority and may appoint or remove managers and control institution-level settings.

At the center of the project is marginalia.

Marginalia is not an accessory attached to a book record. It is a first-class record of a reader’s experience with a book. That experience is represented by a reading session: one reader, one book, at a particular period in time, with its own annotations, bookmarks, and notes.

A reader may have only one open reading session at a time. They may close it when that pass through the book is complete, or leave it open to return later. When they reread the book, they may begin another session and layer that new experience over the earlier ones. The first pass is preserved; the second pass does not overwrite it.

The marginalia belongs to the reader. The library may store it, organize it, and display it, but it does not claim ownership merely because it hosts the data. Readers must be able to bring their marginalia into the library and take it away again. Their reading history should survive changes in library access wherever possible.

This requirement shapes the meaning of a book inside the system. One imported EPUB is one fixed, immutable asset. It has no in-place editions or revisions. Its catalog metadata may be corrected, but the underlying file is never replaced because annotation CFIs refer to the exact structure of that asset. A different edition or corrected EPUB is therefore a different book and must be imported as a new object.

The library server does not render EPUBs or interpret their CFIs. It parses enough metadata to import and catalog an asset safely, then treats the EPUB itself as opaque. The server owns identity, storage, access, sessions, marginalia, and portability.

Rendering belongs to the separate Second Pass Reader web client. The client understands EPUB structure, resolves CFIs, displays the book, and coordinates the reader’s active session and annotations. Its rendering layer contains the complexity of the external EPUB library so that the rest of the application communicates through narrower, app-specific contracts.

Because the Second Pass Reader web client understands the book’s contents, it also serves as the translation boundary for annotations imported from other reading systems. The EPUB becomes a Rosetta stone: foreign locations and annotation formats are interpreted against the actual asset, translated into Second Pass Library’s marginalia model, and then stored by the server without requiring the server itself to understand how EPUB rendering works.

Across both applications, the same architectural idea repeats: each layer has a responsibility and communicates through explicit boundaries. Server protocol details remain inside an SDK. Orchestrators coordinate pages and major surfaces. Regions assemble their own components. Components render and interact with data. Props carry information and commands downward; events and callbacks carry intent upward. Shared objects are promoted only when their reuse becomes real and their purpose remains clear.

The project is therefore not merely an EPUB server or reader. It is a library built around the continuity of a person’s relationship with a book: a place where books are preserved as stable objects, reading experiences accumulate rather than disappear, and the notes in the margins continue to belong to the reader.
